# Card design

Use these principles when selecting and writing cards.

## Evidence-backed principles

### Require retrieval

The front should make the learner produce an answer from memory. A prompt that
only asks the learner to recognize or reread text gives weaker retrieval
practice.

Retrieval practice improves delayed retention compared with repeated study.
Feedback still matters: the answer should make it easy to correct an error.

### Let Anki space later retrievals

Durable learning needs retrieval across separate sessions. The card author
defines the cue and answer; Anki controls the review interval. Do not encode a
manual review schedule in the card text.

### Match the prompt to the intended use

If the goal is factual recall, ask for the fact. If the goal is explanation,
diagnosis, comparison, or application, ask a question that practices that use.
Repeated tests can support transfer, but transfer depends on what the learner
retrieves and how the later task relates to it.

### Retrieve successfully more than once

One correct answer is not mastery. Successive relearning combines successful
retrieval with later spaced retrieval. The skill creates material for this
process; it does not decide that one review is enough.

## Design heuristics

The rules below are practical heuristics, not universal experimental laws.

- One main target: avoid a question that requires an arbitrary list of several
  unrelated answers.
- Precise cue: the question should have one intended answer from the learner's
  knowledge base.
- Minimum sufficient answer: remove detail that is not needed to answer the
  question, but retain a short explanation when it prevents rote confusion.
- Necessary context: include the domain, conditions, units, direction, or time
  frame needed to disambiguate the answer.
- No answer leakage: do not repeat a distinctive answer word in the question.
- Stable wording: avoid references such as “the previous section,” “this,” or
  “the diagram above” unless the required content is inside the card.
- Useful direction: do not reverse a cause-and-effect or many-to-one relation
  unless the reverse question is also meaningful.
- Selective coverage: prefer important, durable knowledge over transient detail
  or facts that are easier to look up than remember.

## Choosing a prompt

Use prompts such as:

- Definition: “What does X mean in Y?”
- Discrimination: “How does X differ from Y?”
- Mechanism: “Why does X cause Y under Z?”
- Procedure: “What is the next step after X, and why?”
- Application: “Given conditions X, which rule applies?”
- Error prevention: “What common mistake occurs when X, and how is it avoided?”

Do not force source material into these templates. Choose the retrieval act the
learner will need later.

## Quality check

For every card, ask:

1. Is this knowledge worth retrieving later?
2. Can the question be answered without reading nearby prose?
3. Is there one clear target?
4. Does the answer fully resolve the question?
5. Is the card type appropriate?
6. Does another card already test the same thing?
7. Did the source support every claim in the answer?

## Research basis

- Roediger and Karpicke, 2006,
  [Test-Enhanced Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x):
  retrieval improved long-term retention compared with repeated study.
- Cepeda et al., 2008,
  [Spacing Effects in Learning](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0kp5q19x):
  the useful spacing interval depends on how long the material must be retained.
- Butler, 2010,
  [Repeated Testing Produces Superior Transfer](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019902):
  repeated retrieval supported retention and transfer in the studied tasks.
- Rawson and Dunlosky, 2022,
  [Successive Relearning](https://doi.org/10.1177/09637214221100484):
  review of practice that combines successful retrieval with spaced relearning.
